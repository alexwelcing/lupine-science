A Seven-Tier Map of MLIP Error, by Property Type (July 2026)

A map, drawn in July 2026, of MLIP error by property type: where one correction held in a narrow case, where corrections failed, and the order we planned to test next

  • Originally published July 20, 2026
  • ·
  • Updated November 11, 2026
  • ·
  • Published

We spent two campaigns measuring where foundation models fail hardest — barriers and interfaces, the observables where errors run to electron-volts and every cheap fix dies. People reasonably ask why we started at the deep end. The answer: a boundary is only visible from the far side. Now that we've walked it, the whole ladder is in view — where a correction has held in a narrow case, where only boundary results can speak, and the order of effort we planned for the rounds ahead. This is that ladder, with the receipts.

Why map the hard end first

Barriers and interfaces are where MLIPs drifted farthest in our tests: 135.0–242.5 meV of barrier error against a 40 meV gate across four foundation models, and adsorption errors up to +25.6 eV. We deliberately ran there first, because a boundary is easiest to see from the hard side. The result, honestly recorded: no low-dimensional correction survived on that error field in our tests. But the same measurement produced something more useful than a win: the complete map of difficulty, from observables where one correction has held in a narrow case to domains where the only honest answer is abstention.

The ladder, as measured

Tier 1 — statics (lattice constants, volumes, relaxed bulk). In Round 3, a direction-gated correction cut lattice-constant error from 1.60% to 0.33% (ionics) and 1.75% to 0.74% (perovskites) on unseen candidates, inside a trial whose gates failed overall. Update, 2026-11-11: in Round 4 the preregistered lattice- and elastic-constant correction failed in both groups (ionics 0/4; perovskites 0/1 after a post-measurement amendment), and its lattice-constant gain did not reach significance; the Tier-1 correction is not established. The error structure is small and one-sided, and the runtime cost is trivial.

Tier 2 — elastic response (bulk modulus, elastic constants). Harder, and we mark it honestly: our own kill condition fired on B0. The theorem work here is boundary theorems — classification of which cells may correct and which may not — not correction laws.

Tier 3 — surface energies. On nine fcc metals, a smooth error field predicted a blind facet for three of four models, and an energy-only correction beside one model cut that facet’s error from 9.7% to 1.5% (nickel) at about 15% runtime overhead. A correction is possible here because the field is smooth enough to have an inverse; it has been shown for one model, two metals and one facet.

Tier 4 — defect formation energies. Cross-model spreads of 0.5–1.8 eV with sign instability. Corrections need per-defect anchoring; the theorem work is scope restriction.

Tier 5 — transition-state barriers. Where we just spent two campaigns. The verdict: energy levels fail beyond rescue by any cheap correction we tried, but energy geometry — saddle locations — was right on 82–86% of paths (89–93% within one image). The boundary results point to one route worth testing: sparse DFT anchors at model-chosen extrema.

Tier 6 — interface adsorption. Structured underbinding on our panel, up to +25.6 eV; frozen corrections made the holdout worse. Only on-policy corrections (fitted during the search itself) might have a chance here, and none has been tested.

Tier 7 — magnetic and excited-state properties. No measurement path exists in current models. Not a correction domain — an abstention domain, by construction.

The rule that keeps the ladder from fighting itself

Non-competition is structural: each correction family may act only where the family below it provably cannot. Level shifts are dead everywhere — a theorem kills them (barrier_shift_invariant), so they never compete. Slope corrections are cleared only by sign stability. Environment-field corrections apply only where a smooth field exists. Anchors are priced exactly: two extrema, exactness, and the model demoted to geometry guide. And a single wobble bound referees all of it: no information-free correction can beat the model’s profile wobble. These are Lean-checked statements about the arithmetic of corrections; whether a given correction helps on real materials is still decided by measurement.

What the next rounds look like

As planned in July 2026:

Round easy-first: Tier 1. The goal was a model-independent runtime gain on relaxed-structure observables, with overhead measured per cell. Update, 2026-11-11: Round 4’s preregistered test of the Tier-1 correction failed; see Round 4.

Sparse-DFT pilot: Tier 5’s route — models guide, DFT measures only at model-chosen extrema. In simulation on the locked reference profile, that reproduced barriers at 1.2–9.4 meV mean error at about 7 evaluations per path. The memorandum of theory was frozen before results (PDF). Update, 2026-11-11: when we ran it with GPAW, the preregistered gate against the published references failed; the check that passed is same-engine only, and the pilot was about 700 meV off the references. See Shared DFT Anchors.

The boundary at each step: when a round hits the tier where its correction family provably can’t act, the boundary is already written down — that’s what the Lean layer is for.
