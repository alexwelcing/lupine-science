Two billion people face water scarcity.
Air pollution is linked to millions of premature deaths every year.
And solving both problems depends on better materials: membranes that reject salt,
sorbents that pull water from dry air, and catalysts that destroy pollutants.
Here is the catch: the AI models screening those materials make their biggest errors
exactly where the chemistry happens—in tiny pores, exposed metal centers, and single-atom sites.
These atoms have fewer neighbors, or lower coordination,
than the bulk crystals in the training data.
In that low-coordination zone, universal machine-learning potentials
can soften the energy landscape by 15 to 60 percent.
Barriers look too easy. Binding looks wrong.
A raw screen can rank the flashy failure above the material that actually works.
For water, that can mean choosing the wrong desalination pore,
or overestimating how long an atmospheric-water framework will survive hydrolysis.
It can mean missing the pathway that separates lithium from magnesium.
Different devices, same atomic-scale ranking error.
For air, it means false promises in cold-start NOx catalysts, soot filters,
and systems that oxidize formaldehyde or benzene.
Their performance also lives at under-coordinated active sites,
where an underestimated barrier becomes pollution that reaches the street—or your living room.
Lupine measures that coordination error, adds analytic correction forces at runtime,
and verifies where the answer is supported.
Across 36 blind model-and-material combinations, corrected predictions reached a correlation of 0.906,
with zero adjustable parameters, while staying about 100,000 times faster than DFT.
So this is not one fix for one molecule.
It is one trust layer for the pores and active sites shaping every sip, every breath,
and the materials we cannot afford to misrank.
